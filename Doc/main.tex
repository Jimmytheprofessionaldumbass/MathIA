    \section{Introduction}
        In Vex robotics, there are sensors for quickly measuring locations for accurate repeated robotic automationos. And the most useful one is an IMU. An inertial measurement unit (IMU) is a sensor that measures acceleration at a high rate. Whenever you need to know the precise location of something quickly, integrating accelerometer data twice to find displacement outpaces GPS solutions. They rely on the property that the area under a accelerometer graph can provide enough data used to compute the displacement of the object. In this investigation we will explore numerical and analytical integration of the accuracy of accelerometer data recorded on a phone accelerometer and comparing with video recorded displacement.

\section{Integrating to velocity}
We will need to integrate once to get velocity:
\begin{equation}
    v(t) = \int \left(a\cdot\sin\left(bt+c\right)-d\right)\diff t
\end{equation}
where a, b, c, and d are constants. Rearranged, we get
\begin{equation}
    v(t) = a \int \sin(bt+c)\diff t - \int d \diff t
\end{equation}
Using u-substitution
\begin{align}
    u &= bt+c \\
    \frac{\diff u}{\diff t} &= b \\
    \diff t &= \frac{\diff u}{b}
\end{align}
plugging in u and dt:
\begin{align}
    v(t) &= a \int \sin(bt+c)\diff t - \int d \diff t \\
    &= a \int \sin(u) \frac{1}{b} \diff u - \int d \diff t \\
    &= \frac{a}{b}\int \sin(u)  \diff u - \int d \diff t \\
    &= -\frac{a}{b}\cos(u) - \int d \diff t \\
    &= -\frac{a}{b}\cos(bt+c) - dt + C_{1}
\end{align}
we get our velocity time equation

\begin{equation}
    v(t) = -\frac{a}{b}\cos(bt+c) - dt + C_{1}
\end{equation}


In kinematics, the constant $C_{1}$ is the initial velocity, and since we know that our initial velocity is 0, we can ignore $C_{1}$ and set it to zero...is what I would say if I didn't pay enough attention.
Because if we find the constant $C_{1}$ of our modeled function when initial velocity $v(0)$ is actually $= 0$
\begin{align}
    v(0) &= -\frac{a}{b}\cos(\left(0\right)b+c) + C_{1} = 0 \\
    \frac{a}{b}\cos(c) &= C_{1} = 0.0209628448456 \\
\end{align}
It is not actually zero! That's because since our initial acceleration (defined by $a\sin(bt+c) + d$) depends on both c and d to determine initial acceleration, and even when $d=0$, $c \neq 0$ which means our initial acceleration isn't actually zero, but why would that mean our initial velocity $C_{1}$ isn't zero then?

In this case, we shouldn't focus on $a$ because that is solely the amplitude while b and c determine the horizontal characteristics of the sine wave function such as horizontal compression and phase shift. and phase shift in particular is responsible for where on the wave we start, and even if the vertical shift is 0, we could still start at the crest of the sine function, which would mean our initial velocity isn't zero.

\section{A conversation with $C_{1}$}


Okay. The problem is that if we have a $C_{1}$ that is not 0, then this would mean that our displacement graph will drift because it means that the area of the graph is either more positive or more negative, which adds up over time. How do we make sense of $C_{1}$ then? And if I remember that the phone ended in the same place it started, then why would $C_{1}$ be nonzero? There's the obvious answer of uncertainty at play, but what does $C_{1}\neq 0$ imply about our mathematical model?

Earlier, we solved
\begin{align}
    v(0) &= -\frac{a}{b}\cos(\left(0\right)b+c) + C_{1} = 0 \\
    \frac{a}{b}\cos(c) &= C_{1} = 0.0209628448456 \\
\end{align}

By adding $C_{1}$ here it assumes that the antiderivative $F(x) = -\frac{a}{b}\cos(\left(0\right)b+c)$ (without the $C_{1}$) is unable to be zero, meaning that we assume integrating the acceleration model wouldn't result in the initial velocity being zero $\int a(t)\Big(t=0\Big) = v(0)=0$ and that we need an additional term $C_{1}$ to vertical shift $v(t) = F(t)$ by $C_{1}$ such that the initial velocity of the velocity function is zero.
So the mathematical reason why $C_{0} \neq 0$ is because $F(0)\neq 0$ meaning $F(x) = -\frac{a}{b}\cos(\left(0\right)b+c) \neq 0$. If we do want to make $C_{1} = 0$, this means we need $-\frac{a}{b}\cos(\left(0\right)b+c) = 0$. And since we know that $a$ and $b$ are responsible for the horizontal/vertical dilation, we know it cannot be zero, so we ignore that for now. Leaving us with
\begin{equation}
    \cos(\left(0\right)b+c) = 0
    \cos(c) = 0
    c = \frac{\pi}{2}
\end{equation}
which means in order for $C_{1} = 0$ we need $c=\frac{\pi}{2}$. Which is still fine, because acceleration could start a a non zero value, it just means that it experienced a force. Which makes sense because for our velocity function

But it does raise an implication. It implies that the phase shift $c$ is able to impact the drift $C_{1}$ of the integrated velocity function if the velocity function has a condition $v(0)=0$. It makes sense because the antiderivative of sin(x+c) is -cos(x+c), so at the end, we are just saying that by changing (the phase shift of the original function), we are going to start at different initial velocities.

\subsection{Integrating to displacement}

\begin{align}
    s(t) &= \int \left(-\frac{a}{b}\cos(bt+c) - dt + C_{1}\right) \diff t \\
    s(t) &= -\frac{a}{b} \int\cos(bt+c)\diff t - \int dt\diff t + \int C_{1}\diff t \\
    s(t) &= -\frac{a}{b} \int\cos(bt+c)\diff t - \int dt\diff t + \int C_{1}\diff t \\
\end{align}
Using the same u-substitution:
\begin{align}
    s(t) &= -\frac{a}{b} \int\cos(u)\frac{\diff u}{b} - \int dt\diff t + \int C_{1}\diff t \\
    s(t) &= -\frac{a}{b^{2}} \sin(u) - \int dt\diff t + \int C_{1}\diff t + C_{2} \\
    s(t) &= -\frac{a}{b^{2}} \sin(bt+c) - \int dt\diff t + \int C_{1}\diff t + C_{2} \\
    s(t) &= -\frac{a}{b^{2}} \sin(bt+c) - \frac{dt^{2}}{2} + C_{1}t + C_{2} \\
\end{align}
and since this data is filmed as relative displacement from original point (seen as 0) we need to find the value of $C_{2}$ such that $s(0)=0$.
\begin{align}
    s(0) &= -\frac{a}{b^{2}} \sin(b\left(0\right)+c) - \frac{d\left(0\right)^{2}}{2} + C_{1}\left(0\right) + C_{2} = 0 \\
    0 &= -\frac{a}{b^{2}} \sin(b\left(0\right)+c) + C_{2} \\
    \frac{a}{b^{2}} \sin(b\left(0\right)+c) &= C_{2} = 0.000695067592524 \\
\end{align}
Again we will keep $C_{2}=0.000695067592524 $ in mind.
Resulting in a final function $s(t)$:
\begin{equation}
    s(t) = -\frac{a}{b^{2}} \sin(bt+c) - \frac{dt^{2}}{2} + C_{1}t + C_{2}
\end{equation}

\section{Conclusion}
We used two methods that both tries to find the displacement-time graph using a given accelerometer-time data, numerically and analytically.
For the numerical approach, we have a lot of points that make up a graph when you connect the points. And since there are points, we can use geometry (although it is a lot of geometry) to find the area under the curve made by said points. We centered the data by subtracting each point by the mean of every point's y-value before integrating it numerically to reduce drift during integrated graphs.
For the Analytical approach, we fitted a sinusoidal regression on the same accelerometer-time data and then we used algebra and integral calculus to integrate twice to find the displacement-time graph.

\subsection{Evaluation}
While the numerical method follows every accelerometer data point, the analytical method first tries to model it, which makes it a continuous smooth curve and then integrates analytically.

In both cases, a tiny imperfection can get amplified, resulting in drift, but in different ways. In the numerical way, the data is collected in real life and have sensor uncertainties, meaning sometimes it should in theory be one accelerometer value that would result in an integration such that the velocity can start at zero and end at zero. But instead, it is just a little higher or lower, making the mean acceleration higher or lower, and when the mean acceleration is higher or lower, there is a tiny constant change in velocity over time, which results in drift over time.  And if you integrate it again, that tiny change gets ever more prominent as time passes. We corrected this drift by making sure the mean is centered before we integrate each time so that the integrated data will not have drift. But there is also sensor uncertainty, which adds more opportunities for drift over time. 
While numerical methods accumulate uncertainty over time as a result of sensor accuracy. Modeling data with a sinusoidal rergression still can drift, but due to a more mathematical reason. For the equation we decided to model, we already established the fact that the constant $d$ in $a\sin(bx+c)+d$ refers to the mean in terms of its modeled data, and when you integrate any constant, it increases its degree, and making it more impactful over time. However, a more interesting case is when we encounter the first constant $C_{1}$ after integration, which turns out to be determined by the phase of our sinusoidal model $c$. Which intuitively doesn't make sense; why would the phase of an oscillatory function determine the magnitude of drift of its integrated velocity function? Well, its because of our condition that our our initial velocity is 0, but the part in the modeled sine function in which we started recording the data when integrated doesn't have its initial velocity equal to 0, so we have to move it either up or down to compensate, which adds the drift once we integrate again to find displacement.
