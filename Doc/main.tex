    \section{Introduction}
        In Vex robotics, there are sensors for quickly measuring locations for accurate repeated robotic automationos. And the most useful one is an IMU. An inertial measurement unit (IMU) is a sensor that measures acceleration at a high rate. Whenever you need to know the precise location of something quickly, double integrating accelerometer data outpaces GPS solutions. They rely on the property that the area under a accelerometer graph can provide enough data used to compute the displacement of the object. In this investigation we will explore numerical and analytical integration of the accuracy of accelerometer data recorded on a phone accelerometer and comparing with video recorded displacement.

\section{How to find displacement from acceleration graph}
    Recall that in kinematics acceleration is defined as the derivative of velocity
\begin{equation}
    a = \frac{\diff v}{\diff t}
\end{equation}

To find the derivative from first principals, we use limits:

\begin{equation}
    \lim_{h \to 0}  \frac{v(t+h) - v(t)}{h}
\end{equation}
and $v(t+h) - v(t)$ essentially means the change in velocity over a short period of time $h$. And as $h$ approaches 0, it becomes an increasingly more accurate representation of the derivative of acceleration, velocity. And this means that for our data to be accurate, the sample rate has to be high.

Recall the fact that velocity is the integral of acceleration and displacement is the integral of velocity. Integral is the area under a curve. For continuous functions, they are an approximateted by dividing the function into many "rectangles" like shown below where the height of a specific rectangle at a specific point of the function is the y value of the function like shown below.

\section{Modeling}

I decided to compare integrating numerically against analytically; I want to know if there is a difference if I first modeled it with a sine equation and then integrated the equation algebraicly rather than numerically using geometry like I did before.

Desmos has a tool for fitting data into many types of equations. In particular, we are looking at a sinulsoidal regression
\begin{equation}
    y = a\sin(bx+c)+d
\end{equation}
Plugging into desmos for sinusoidal regression we get:
\begin{equation}

\section{Integrating to velocity}
We will need to integrate once to get velocity:
\begin{equation}
    v(t) = \int \left(a\cdot\sin\left(bt+c\right)-d\right)\diff t
\end{equation}
where a, b, c, and d are constants. Rearranged, we get
\begin{equation}
    v(t) = a \int \sin(bt+c)\diff t - \int d \diff t
\end{equation}
Using u-substitution
\begin{align}
    u &= bt+c \\
    \frac{\diff u}{\diff t} &= b \\
    \diff t &= \frac{\diff u}{b}
\end{align}
plugging in u and dt:
\begin{align}
    v(t) &= a \int \sin(bt+c)\diff t - \int d \diff t \\
    &= a \int \sin(u) \frac{1}{b} \diff u - \int d \diff t \\
    &= \frac{a}{b}\int \sin(u)  \diff u - \int d \diff t \\
    &= -\frac{a}{b}\cos(u) - \int d \diff t \\
    &= -\frac{a}{b}\cos(bt+c) - dt + C_{1}
\end{align}
we get our velocity time equation

\begin{equation}
    v(t) = -\frac{a}{b}\cos(bt+c) - dt + C_{1}
\end{equation}


In kinematics, the constant $C_{1}$ is the initial velocity, and since we know that our initial velocity is 0, we can ignore $C_{1}$ and set it to zero...is what I would say if I didn't pay enough attention.
Because if we find the constant $C_{1}$ of our modeled function when initial velocity $v(0)$ is actually $= 0$
\begin{align}
    v(0) &= -\frac{a}{b}\cos(\left(0\right)b+c) + C_{1} = 0 \\
    \frac{a}{b}\cos(c) &= C_{1} = 0.0209628448456 \\
\end{align}
It is not actually zero! That's because since our initial acceleration (defined by $a\sin(bt+c) + d$) depends on both c and d to determine initial acceleration, and even when $d=0$, $c \neq 0$ which means our initial acceleration isn't actually zero, but why would that mean our initial velocity $C_{1}$ isn't zero then?


That's because $C_{1}$ is dependent on the other terms a, b, and c. But still, it doesn't mean that $C_{1}$ doesn't depend on initial velocity, it may not depend on d, but d isn't the only value that determines initial velocity. 

As we mentioned before, removing d from our sinusoidal regression removes a certain amount of drift; particularly to the extent that our velocity value can still return to zero (come to rest) no matter how large the value t (however long time has passed). But still, even without d: in order for $a(t)=a\sin(bt+c)$'s integrated velocity function $-\frac{a}{b}\cos(bt+c) + C_{1}$ to end at zero, we must also define the range of the function such that we end at $v(t) = -\frac{a}{b}\cos(bt+c) + C_{1} = 0$. In this case, we shouldn't focus on $a$ because that is solely the amplitude while b and c determine the horizontal characteristics of the sine wave function such as horizontal compression and phase shift. and phase shift in particular is responsible for where on the wave we start, and even if the vertical shift is 0, we could still start at the crest of the sine function, which would mean our initial velocity isn't zero. 

So now we figured out that a $d>0$ will result in the velocity function $v(t) = -\frac{a}{b}\cos(bt+c) - dt + C_{1}$ to never reach zero as time $t$ increases, and setting $d=0$ will make it so we can still end up at zero. It doesn't exactly still imply that our displacement will go back to zero.

. Regardless, we know if we continue integrating with an additional constant, its bound to add drift, because $\int c_{1} dx = c_{1}x+c$ and as x increases, the y value also goes up. But this time we have no choice but to keep it because.

$C_{1}$ adds drift to our equation. but could it also help us model the case where my end displacement isn't exactly the same spot? If we have the end position be different, then this will be determined by the mean. if it is zero then it is the exact place. But if it isn't, then THIS is what determines the change in displacement between the beginning and the end, not c. 

\section{A conversation with $C_{1}$}


Okay. the problem is that if we have a $C_{1}$ that is not 0, then this would mean that our displacement graph will drift because it means that the area of the graph is either more positive or more negative, which adds up over time. How do we make sense of $C_{1}$ then? And if I remember that the phone ended in the same place it started, then why would $C_{1}$ be nonzero? There's the obvious answer of uncertainty at play, but what does $C_{1}\neq 0$ imply about our mathematical model?

Earlier, we solved
\begin{align}
    v(0) &= -\frac{a}{b}\cos(\left(0\right)b+c) + C_{1} = 0 \\
    \frac{a}{b}\cos(c) &= C_{1} = 0.0209628448456 \\
\end{align}

By adding $C_{1}$ here it assumes that the antiderivative $F(x) = -\frac{a}{b}\cos(\left(0\right)b+c)$ (without the $C_{1}$) is unable to be zero, meaning that we assume integrating the acceleration model wouldn't result in the initial velocity being zero $\int a(t)\Big_{t=0} = v(0)=0$ and that we need an additional term $C_{1}$ to vertical shift $v(t) = F(t)$ by $C_{1}$ such that the initial velocity of the velocity function is zero.
So the mathematical reason why $C_{0} \neq 0$ is because $F(0)\neq 0$ meaning $F(x) = -\frac{a}{b}\cos(\left(0\right)b+c) \neq 0$. If we do want to make $C_{1} = 0$, this means we need $-\frac{a}{b}\cos(\left(0\right)b+c) = 0$. And since we know that $a$ and $b$ are responsible for the horizontal/vertical dilation, we know it cannot be zero, so we ignore that for now. Leaving us with
\begin{equation}
    \cos(\left(0\right)b+c) = 0
    \cos(c) = 0
    c = \frac{\pi}{2}
\end{equation}
which means in order for $C_{1} = 0$ we need $c=\frac{\pi}{2}$. Which makes sense because for our velocity function

\begin{align}

\section{Comparison}
We used two methods that both tries to find the displacement-time graph using a given accelerometer-time data, numerically and analytically.
For the numerical approach, we have a lot of points that make up a graph when you connect the points. And since there are points, we can use geometry (although it is a lot of geometry) to find the area under a curve. We centered the data by subtracting each point by the mean of every point's y-value before integrating it numerically to reduce drift during integrated graphs.
For the Analytical approach, we fitted a sinusoidal regression on the same accelerometer-time data and then we used algebra and integral calculus to integrate twice to find the displacement-time graph.
